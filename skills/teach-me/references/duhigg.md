# Duhigg, The Power of Habit, distilled for a study-habit coach

Charles Duhigg's *The Power of Habit* (2012) argues that habits follow a neurological loop of cue, routine and reward, and that anyone who understands the loop can rebuild their own habits. The edition read is *O Poder do Hábito*, Editora Objetiva, Rio de Janeiro, 2012, translated by Rafael Mantovani (ISBN 978-85-390-0425-6). This distillate covers the prologue, chapters 1 to 5 and the appendix, and chapter numbers cited below follow that edition.

## Prologue: The habit cure

- Treat the learner's study habit as a single pattern to be rebuilt, not as a personality trait to be fixed (prologue). The book opens with a woman who transformed her whole life after committing to change one habit, and the scientists who studied her found that the old neural patterns were still there but had been overridden by new ones.
- Tell the learner that a habit is a choice they once made deliberately, then stopped thinking about, and now repeat without deciding (prologue). This is the book's working definition, and it implies that the choice can be made again.
- Point out that most daily actions are habits rather than decisions, so a study habit competes with automatic patterns rather than with reasoned intentions (prologue). The book cites a Duke study finding that over 40 percent of daily actions are habitual.
- Frame the work as understanding the mechanism first, then changing it, and warn the learner that change is possible but rarely quick or easy (prologue). The prologue's closing claim is that habits can be changed once their workings are understood, not that they can be changed effortlessly.

## Chapter 1: The habit loop

- Ask the learner to name the cue, the routine and the reward of their current study attempt before proposing a change (ch. 1). The book describes a three-stage loop in which a cue tells the brain to go automatic, a routine runs, and a reward tells the brain whether the loop is worth remembering.
- Explain that the brain converts a repeated sequence into a single chunk, and that this is why the first sessions feel effortful and later ones do not (ch. 1). Rats learning a maze at MIT showed intense brain activity at first, then near silence once the route became automatic, with the basal ganglia taking over.
- Keep the cue and the surrounding conditions constant across sessions, because habits are fragile and break when the cue shifts (ch. 1). An amnesiac patient could walk home only when the landmarks on his route were unchanged; roadwork or fallen branches left him lost even close to home.
- Warn the learner that an old habit never disappears, so a lapse is the old loop reasserting itself rather than a failure of character (ch. 1). A researcher reports that rats trained to a maze, then retrained, immediately revert when the original reward location returns.
- Design the new study habit to be stronger than the competing one rather than trying to delete the competing one (ch. 1). The book says a new routine can push a harmful pattern into the background but cannot erase it.
- Look for cues in five kinds of places, since almost any stimulus can serve, including a place, a time, an emotion, a train of thought or particular people (ch. 1). The book lists these as the range of cues researchers have found.
- Accept that the reward can be emotional, such as pride or praise, rather than material (ch. 1). The patient's daughter got him to keep hospital sensors attached by having nurses praise him repeatedly for helping science.
- Notice that a habit can be emotional and can run its course even after the learner forgets what started it, so address frustration loops around study directly (ch. 1). The amnesiac patient stayed angry after forgetting the trigger, and a ten-second change in the trigger prevented the anger entirely.
- Expect that even small environmental changes can break an unwanted loop, and use that deliberately, for example by removing distracting items from the study space (ch. 1). The patient's wife stopped his repeated fatty breakfasts by not keeping bacon in the fridge and by placing a salad beside his chair.

## Chapter 2: The craving brain

- Pick one simple, obvious cue and one clearly defined reward for the study habit before anything else (ch. 2). These are the two rules the advertiser behind Pepsodent credited for turning toothbrushing into a national daily ritual, and the book says exercise and diet studies confirm them.
- Do not stop at cue and reward; help the learner build an anticipation of the reward, because only a craving makes the loop self-sustaining (ch. 2). Monkey experiments showed that after enough repetitions the brain's reward signal fired at the sight of the cue, before the juice arrived, and that once this anticipation existed the monkeys ignored distractions.
- Ask the learner to spend a moment imagining the reward before starting a session, so the cue begins to trigger the craving and not just the routine (ch. 2). The book suggests thinking about the post-workout smoothie or endorphin rush as a way to let a craving grow.
- Choose a reward the learner actually wants, and check whether the reward they say they want is the one they crave (ch. 2). Pepsodent's real hook was the cool tingling sensation users came to miss, not the promised beautiful teeth, and competitors who copied only the advertising copy failed.
- Make sure the cue is noticeable to the learner, not only to you (ch. 2). Febreze failed at first because the people with the smelliest homes could no longer smell them, so the intended cue never fired.
- Attach the study routine to the end of an existing routine and position the reward as a small celebration of finishing (ch. 2). Febreze succeeded once it was reframed as the pleasant final touch after cleaning a room, with the cue being the completed chore.
- Build in a sensory or visible signal that the work happened, since people need evidence that the routine worked (ch. 2). Foam in shampoo and tingling in toothpaste exist only to signal effectiveness, and the book says that signal is what sustains the habit.
- Remove cues for competing habits during study, for example by silencing notifications (ch. 2). The book notes that turning off a phone's vibration removes the cue and lets people work for hours without checking.
- Use a sense of accomplishment from tracking progress as a legitimate reward (ch. 2). In a study of habitual exercisers, one group kept going for the good feeling and another for the sense of achievement that came from recording their performance.
- When the learner is tempted to skip, have them focus on the craving for the chosen reward (ch. 2). Successful dieters in a national registry kept a specific reward in mind and cultivated the craving until it outweighed the temptation.

## Chapter 3: The golden rule of habit change

- To change an existing bad habit, keep its cue and its reward and replace only the routine (ch. 3). The book calls this the golden rule and shows it in a football coach's method, in Alcoholics Anonymous and in clinical habit reversal.
- Do not try to make the learner drop a habit outright; give them a replacement behavior that serves the same craving (ch. 3). The book says attempts to quit snacking or smoking usually fail unless a new routine satisfies the old cue and craving.
- Have the learner list what triggers their procrastination or avoidance, and say it aloud or in writing to someone else (ch. 3). Researchers describe AA's inventory and confession steps as, in practice, a way to make people identify their cues and recall the moments things went wrong.
- Ask what the avoidance behavior actually gives the learner, because the reward is often relief, distraction, company or escape rather than the surface activity (ch. 3). Alcoholics in the book crave relief and companionship more than intoxication, and a neurologist notes that these cravings live in different brain regions from physical pleasure.
- Reduce the number of things the learner must decide at the start of a session by turning the first steps into fixed reactions to fixed cues (ch. 3). The coach taught his players to read the same visual keys they already used, but in a set order with a predetermined response, so speed came from not deciding.
- Expect the new routine to take a long time to become automatic and to fail early, and say so in advance (ch. 3). The coach's system took nearly a year to take hold and the team lost easy games first.
- Run awareness training first, by having the learner describe the physical or mental feeling that precedes the unwanted behavior (ch. 3). A therapist treating a chronic nail-biter began by asking what she felt just before her hand went to her mouth, and the book says most people have stopped noticing their triggers.
- Give the learner a tally card and have them mark every time the cue fires, before trying to change anything (ch. 3). The nail-biter came back with 28 marks after a week and was by then highly aware of the sensations that preceded her habit.
- Teach a competing response, a concrete action to perform the instant the cue appears, and rehearse it with the learner (ch. 3). The nail-biter was told to put her hands in her pockets or grab a pencil and then do something that gave the same physical stimulation, and the habit was gone in a month.
- Have the learner mark both the cue and each successful use of the competing response, and reward streaks (ch. 3). The nail-biter tallied ticks and hashes and rewarded herself with a manicure when the numbers improved.
- Be honest that understanding the loop is not the same as changing it, and that real change takes effort and determination (ch. 3, note 3). The author states in a footnote that nobody quits anything simply by sketching their habit loop.
- Prepare the learner for the moment when stress overrides the new routine, because that is where substitution alone fails (ch. 3). Researchers found that recovering alcoholics who had swapped routines still relapsed under major life stress unless something else was present.
- Help the learner believe that change is possible, since belief is what makes a rebuilt loop survive stress (ch. 3). Studies of AA participants found that the belief itself, not any particular religious content, predicted who stayed sober through hard periods.
- Put the learner in contact with other people who are doing the same thing, even if it is one other person (ch. 3). The book says belief is easier inside a community and cites a study of people who transformed their lives, where most changed because a group made change seem possible.
- Encourage the learner to help someone else in the group early on (ch. 3). One AA member credited his eventual success to volunteering to stack chairs in his first weeks back, which he said put him on a different path.
- Remind the learner, under pressure, to fall back on the practiced routine rather than improvise (ch. 3). Players said that in critical moments they reverted to what they knew, and the coach's halftime message before a comeback was simply to trust the system.

## Chapter 4: Keystone habits

- Do not try to fix every study behavior at once; find one habit whose change will pull the others along (ch. 4). The book defines keystone habits as the ones that, once shifted, dislodge and remake other patterns, and describes an executive who transformed a company by focusing on a single metric.
- Choose as the keystone something the learner and everyone around them already agree matters, so there is no argument about it (ch. 4). The executive picked worker safety because neither unions nor management could object to it.
- Build the keystone habit as a loop with a fixed reporting routine, for example a rule that every missed session is logged with a plan to prevent the next miss (ch. 4). The safety program's cue was an injury, its routine was a report to the top within 24 hours plus a prevention plan, and its reward was promotion.
- Look for a keystone habit that forces the learner to build new structures around it, such as a review ritual that requires notes to exist (ch. 4). The 24-hour reporting rule forced the company to build communication channels that later carried ideas about everything.
- Recommend a modest tracking habit and let the learner discover patterns in it, rather than prescribing many changes (ch. 4). In a weight-loss study, people asked only to write down what they ate one day a week began noticing patterns, planning ahead and eventually lost twice as much weight as the rest.
- Warn against overhauling the learner's whole life at once (ch. 4). The book reports that programs which demanded diet, gym, counseling and stair-climbing all at once failed because no single change could take root.
- Use exercise, family meals or making the bed as examples of small habits that correlate with wider improvement, while being clear that the mechanism is unclear (ch. 4). The book says exercise, even once a week, tends to spill over into better eating, more productivity and less stress, without claiming causation.
- Give the learner a short pre-session ritual that has nothing to do with the subject matter but produces calm and focus (ch. 4). A swimming coach built his champion's success on a few routines of relaxation and visualization rather than on controlling every aspect of his life.
- Have the learner visualize a complete successful session in detail, at night and in the morning, until it feels rehearsed (ch. 4). The swimmer replayed a mental tape of the perfect race so often that racing felt mechanical, and the coach could trigger the state with a few words.
- Include failure scenarios in the rehearsal so the learner has a script for setbacks (ch. 4). The swimmer had rehearsed swimming with leaking goggles and counted strokes calmly when it happened in an Olympic final.
- Engineer a chain of small wins before the hard part, so the difficult task arrives as one more step in a pattern of success (ch. 4). The coach said that by race time the swimmer had already succeeded at every earlier stage of the day and winning felt like a natural extension.
- Treat each small win as evidence for the learner that larger achievements are within reach (ch. 4). The book cites organizational research showing small wins have influence out of proportion to their size because each one sets forces in motion that favor the next.
- Accept that small wins do not line up in a straight path and use them as experiments that reveal hidden resources and obstacles (ch. 4). An organizational psychologist describes small wins as scattered miniature experiments rather than orderly steps toward a predefined goal.
- Celebrate the meaning of a win, not the metric (ch. 4). After a short drop in injuries, the executive congratulated staff but told them to celebrate lives saved rather than rules followed, and workers pinned the memo to their lockers.
- Ask why until the root of a study problem appears, and expect the root to lie far from the symptom (ch. 4). The executive traced infant mortality back to inadequate biology training for rural teachers, a cause no health official would have named.
- Help the learner form a small group with a daily check-in, since a keystone habit that creates a culture supplies strength that discipline alone cannot (ch. 4). West Point cadets who survived credited a morning meeting of nine friends who checked on each other, and researchers found that grit, not test scores, predicted graduation.
- Let the keystone habit define values that make later hard choices obvious (ch. 4). The executive fired a senior manager who hid an incident because the culture had made not sharing a chance to learn an unforgivable act.

## Chapter 5: Willpower as a muscle

- Treat willpower as a finite resource that tires with use, and schedule study before draining tasks rather than after (ch. 5). Students who had resisted cookies gave up on an unsolvable puzzle 60 percent sooner than students who had eaten them, and the researcher says over two hundred studies since have found the same.
- Tell the learner that self-discipline predicts academic performance better than IQ, and that the way to increase it is to make it habitual (ch. 5). A study of eighth graders found self-discipline predicted grades and grade improvement while IQ did not.
- Expect willpower gains in one area to spill into others (ch. 5). People enrolled in exercise, money-management or study-skills programs also smoked less, drank less, watched less television and ate better without being told to.
- Have the learner write a specific plan for the moments when quitting is most tempting, naming the cue and the action (ch. 5). Hip-replacement patients who wrote down exactly when, where and how they would walk, including what to do at the moment of worst pain, recovered up to three times faster than those who did not.
- Rehearse those inflection-point plans until they run automatically (ch. 5). Starbucks trains employees to respond to specific cues, such as an angry customer, with a scripted routine, practiced with managers until it is reflexive.
- Give the learner real choices about how and when they study, because willpower depletes faster when people feel they are only following orders (ch. 5). Students told brusquely not to eat cookies performed far worse on a later attention task than students asked politely and given a reason.
- Do not rely on adding a separate self-discipline program after a tiring day (ch. 5). Starbucks found that employees who lacked discipline at work also could not get themselves to attend gym or diet classes afterward.

## Cross-cutting principles

- The habit loop. Every habit is a cue, a routine and a reward, stored in the basal ganglia and run without conscious decision. Breaking a habit into these parts is the first step to changing it (prologue, ch. 1, ch. 3, appendix).
- Craving and anticipation. A cue and a reward are not enough; the loop becomes automatic only when the brain starts expecting the reward at the cue. Cravings are usually invisible to the person until they look for them (ch. 2, ch. 3, appendix).
- The golden rule. To change a habit, keep the cue and the reward and insert a new routine that satisfies the same craving. Habits cannot be erased, only replaced (ch. 1, ch. 3, appendix).
- Belief and community. A replaced routine holds under stress only when the person believes change is possible, and belief grows most easily inside a group, even a group of two (ch. 3, ch. 4).
- Keystone habits. Some habits start chain reactions that remake other habits by producing small wins, by creating structures that let other habits grow and by building cultures that make values clear (prologue, ch. 4, ch. 5).
- Small wins. Modest, concrete successes convince people that bigger achievements are reachable and set the next win in motion, even though they do not add up in a straight line (ch. 4).
- Awareness and recording. Writing down cues, tallying impulses and logging behavior repeatedly turns invisible patterns visible and is itself a habit that spawns others (ch. 3, ch. 4, appendix).
- Planning for inflection points. Deciding in advance what to do at the moment of greatest temptation, and rehearsing it, is how willpower becomes automatic (ch. 4, ch. 5, appendix).
- Fragility of habits. Habits depend on stable cues, and small changes to the environment can break them, which is a risk for good habits and a tool against bad ones (ch. 1, ch. 2).
- Autonomy. Effort feels less costly and lasts longer when the person experiences it as their own choice (ch. 5).

## Terms the book defines

- Habit loop (loop do hábito). The three-part cycle of cue, routine and reward that the brain runs automatically (ch. 1).
- Cue (deixa). The trigger that tells the brain to switch to automatic mode and which habit to use (ch. 1).
- Routine (rotina). The behavior itself, which may be physical, mental or emotional (ch. 1).
- Reward (recompensa). The payoff that tells the brain whether the loop is worth storing (ch. 1).
- Chunking (chunking, agrupamento). The process by which the brain converts a sequence of actions into one automatic block (ch. 1).
- Basal ganglia (gânglios basais). The primitive brain structure that stores habits and takes over as the rest of the brain quiets (ch. 1).
- Craving (anseio). The anticipatory desire for a reward that arises once cue and reward are linked and that drives the loop (ch. 2).
- Golden rule of habit change (Regra de Ouro da mudança de hábito). Keep the same cue and reward, change the routine (ch. 3).
- Keys (chaves). Visual cues that football players read at the start of a play, which the coach repurposed as habit cues (ch. 3).
- Habit reversal training (treinamento de reversão de hábito). A therapy that identifies the cue and reward of a tic or compulsion and installs a competing response (ch. 3).
- Awareness training (treinamento de consciência). The first stage of habit reversal, in which the patient describes what precedes the behavior (ch. 3).
- Competing response (reação concorrente). A replacement action performed when the cue fires that makes the old routine impossible and supplies the same reward (ch. 3).
- Belief (fé). The conviction that change is possible, which the book identifies as the ingredient that makes a replaced habit permanent (ch. 3).
- Addiction versus habit (vício). The book notes that the line is blurry, that physical nicotine dependence lasts only about a hundred hours, and that much of what feels like addiction is habit (ch. 3, note 2).
- Keystone habit (hábito angular). A habit whose change sets off a chain reaction across other habits in a life or organization (prologue, ch. 4).
- Organizational routines (rotinas). The organizational equivalent of individual habits (ch. 4).
- Small wins (pequenas vitórias). Minor concrete successes that build momentum and belief out of proportion to their size (ch. 4).
- Grit (garra). The tendency to keep working through setbacks and plateaus over years, which predicted West Point graduation better than other measures (ch. 4).
- Willpower (força de vontade). Self-discipline, treated by the book as the most important keystone habit for individual success (ch. 5).
- Willpower muscle (músculo da força de vontade). The metaphor for willpower as a resource that tires with use and strengthens with exercise (ch. 5).
- Inflection points (pontos de inflexão). The moments of peak pain, stress or temptation where self-control tends to fail and where a pre-written plan helps most (ch. 5).
- Implementation intentions (intenções de implementação). The psychological term for a written plan that pairs a cue with a chosen routine and reward (appendix).

## Appendix framework as a checklist

The appendix insists there is no single formula, only a framework for experimenting, and that some habits take a long time or never fully settle. Run these four steps with the learner in order.

1. Identify the routine. Ask the learner to state the behavior they want to change or install, in concrete terms, such as leaving the desk mid-afternoon or opening a video instead of the textbook. Then ask what they suspect the cue is and what they suspect the reward is, and write both down as open questions rather than answers (appendix).
2. Experiment with rewards. For several days, when the impulse fires, have the learner try a different substitute each day that tests one hypothesis about the reward, such as a walk with no snack, a snack at the desk, a coffee, or a short chat with a friend. After each one, have them write the first three things that come to mind, set a 15-minute timer, and record whether the urge is still there. Tell them there is no pressure to change yet; they are collecting data. If the urge persists after a substitute, that substitute did not supply the craved reward (appendix).
3. Isolate the cue. Each time the impulse fires, have the learner note five things at that instant, where they are, what time it is, their emotional state, who else is around, and what they did immediately before. After about three days, look for the category that stays constant; that is the cue. Explain that cues hide because there is too much information, and that naming categories in advance is how patterns become visible (appendix).
4. Have a plan. Write an implementation intention in the form of a sentence that names the cue, the new routine and the reward it delivers, and add a reminder such as an alarm at the cue time. Warn the learner that the plan will fail on some days, and that the days it works will feel better by the end. After a few weeks, the routine should run without the reminder (appendix).
